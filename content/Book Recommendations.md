# Introduction

This page is exactly what it sounds like, a place for me to put down books / other resources which I recommend to students of math / computer science. Most of these are standard, but there are some resources on here which I feel don't get recommended nearly enough. I have used (at least portions of) all of the resources on here. I'll also try to explain briefly what I liked about each of these resources and to whom and why I'd recommend them. In general, my tastes tend to skew heavily towards verbose rather than dense. 

(Brief section for fiction at the end!)

# Math

## General Reading

- [An Infinitely Large Napkin](https://venhance.github.io/napkin/Napkin.pdf), Evan Chen. Great introductions to a LOT of topics in math. Good for supplementing other reading with some more intuition, or getting a quick overview of what a field is about and what its highlights and selling points are.

## Algebra

- A Book of Abstract Algebra, Charles Pinter. Very, very gentle introduction to group, ring, and field theory, assuming nothing more than high school math. This might be too gentle of an introduction, depending on your mathematical background, but better too gentle than too intense, in my opinion. This was my first group theory book, and I really enjoyed it.
- [Linear Algebra Done Right](https://linear.axler.net/), Sheldon Axler. This book is somewhat controversial for its abstract, matrices-last approach to linear algebra, but I loved it. It focuses a lot more on understanding what vector spaces and linear maps are rather than manipulating matrices and determinants, and I feel that this leads to a far deeper understanding of the material. Would very much recommend to anyone for a first course in linear algebra.
- Abstract Algebra, Dummit and Foote. Standard textbook for abstract algebra. I mainly used this for ring and field theory and liked those sections. The primary complaint I hear about this book is that it's too verbose, but my preferences heavily skew towards verbose instead of terse, so I didn't find it off-putting.
- Algebra: Chapter 0, Paolo Aluffi. Fantastic book for a second course in / revision of abstract algebra. The book is very well written with a lot of intuition for everything that it talks about. It also takes a category theoretic viewpoint wherever possible, which makes it great for a revision of abstract algebra in my opinion, since material that one already knows is put in a new light. 

## Real Analysis

- Understanding Analysis, Stephen Abbott. This is my go-to recommendation for an introductory analysis text. The book is very well written, with lots of intuition and examples. The exercises are also very fun! I recommended this to many current first-year undergrads at CMI, and in general they seemed to like it too!
- Real Analysis, Royden and Fitzpatrick. Good for more advanced real analysis - I mainly used this for its material on properties of metric spaces (the Baire Category Theorem, the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, etc.) and introductory functional analysis. I found this much easier to read than Rudin!
- Fourier Analysis, Stein and Shakarchi. I loved this. I used the book for a brief introduction to Fourier analysis, and I thought it was very clearly written and digestible. Would unequivocally recommend this to anybody who wants to study Fourier analysis.
